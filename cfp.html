Neuro-Symbolic and Causal Architectures for Reliable AI Agents

In this call Scope Topics Contribution formats Talks and posters Speakers Organizers What to include Review and program Dates and participation

How should we evaluate AI agents?

How can we tell when an AI agent is ready for use beyond a demonstration?

Reasoning to Readiness will bring together researchers, developers, practitioners, and students to study how memory, knowledge, rules, and causal models affect an agent's reliability. We welcome research, case studies, early work, and negative results from simulations, prototypes, pilots, and practical use.

Participants will compare approaches, discuss results, and work through labs. We aim to develop useful guidance on what to test before a pilot, how to report results, and which decisions should remain with people.

AAAI acceptance is pending. Submissions will open after acceptance; expressions of interest are welcome now.

Topics of interest

Contributions may address one or more of the following areas.

01

Cognitive architectures and memory

Memory, learning, planning, and human review; sources and revision of stored knowledge; reuse of skills across tasks; and the effects of learning on earlier capabilities.

02

Neuro-symbolic reasoning and action

Knowledge graphs, ontologies, rules, and executable checks. Explain how these change the agent's decisions, which actions they check or authorize, and how they handle missing or conflicting information.

03

Causal reasoning and decision support

Causal models, interventions, counterfactual questions, and uncertainty. Explain the assumptions behind a conclusion and when the agent should ask for more information or human help.

04

Evaluation and practical use

Integration with existing systems, access permissions, changes in operating conditions, security, error detection, recovery, cost, and staff effort.

Contribution formats

We plan to accept the following contribution formats.

Research and position papers
Long papers: up to 4 pages. Short papers: up to 2 pages. Present a method, study, or research argument, with available results or a clear plan for testing it. Early work and negative results are welcome.
Industry and operational case studies Up to 4 pages
Describe the problem, current practice or comparison, results, and limitations. State whether the work is a simulation, laboratory study, pilot, or production system. Authors from any affiliation may submit cases.
Demonstrations Up to 2 pages
Describe the system and provide instructions for running it or replaying its results. Include an example of a failure, invalid action, or missing information and explain how the system responds.
Poster abstracts Up to 1 page
Summarize the question, approach, available findings, and points for discussion. Accepted abstracts will be presented in the dedicated poster session.
Participation statements Up to 1 page
Explain the problem or experience you would bring to the meeting and a question you would like to discuss. Students and practitioners are encouraged to contribute.

What to include

Describe what you are studying, how you evaluate it, what you have found, and what remains uncertain. For early work, explain the tests you plan to run.

  1. Problem and purpose. Explain the task, intended users, and decisions the agent may make.
  2. Approach and comparison. Describe the learned models, knowledge, rules, checks, or causal models. Where possible, compare the approach with a simpler alternative using similar information and resources.
  3. Results and limitations. Report test conditions, sample sizes, uncertainty, and failures. Include response time, cost, and human effort where relevant.
  4. Human review. Explain which decisions need approval and what happens when the system needs help or must stop.

Example: supplier delays and approval decisions

Compare an agent with and without an approval check. Test outdated supplier information and a missing approval, separately and together. Record unauthorized actions, requests for human help, response time, and the decisions that still require an operations manager.

Share only material you have permission to release. If data or code cannot be shared, explain the restriction and provide an anonymized example, a simulation, or a description of the tests where possible.

Review and discussion

Reviews will consider relevance, clarity, the methods and results appropriate to the submission, and its value for discussion. We plan at least two reviews by reviewers without conflicts for each paper and case study. Early work and position papers will be assessed on their question, argument, and proposed tests. Poster abstracts will be assessed using the same criteria at a level appropriate to their length.

Presentations and posters will contribute to discussion throughout the meeting. Lab 1, on Monday, March 22, 14:00–15:30, will focus on designing an agent for supplier delays and approval decisions. Lab 2, on Tuesday, March 23, 11:00–12:30, will test outdated information, missing approvals, and tool failures. Participants will also compare their findings with a case from service operations. We plan to summarize useful evaluation methods, examples of failures, and open research questions.
